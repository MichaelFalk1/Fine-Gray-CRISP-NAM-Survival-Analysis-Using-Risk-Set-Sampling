\section{Background and Motivation}

The modern world is currently navigating a highly data driven era, where the volume, velocity, and variety of information generated across multiple domains continue to grow at an unprecedented, exponential rate \cite{Laney2001}. From global financial transactions and sensor networks to electronic health records and longitudinal patient tracking, there is now a digital footprint for almost every human interaction. While this vast availability of data has paved the way for highly sophisticated, deep learning analytical models \cite{LeCun2015} , it has simultaneously introduced massive computational challenges. Training algorithms to process and extract meaningful patterns from these sprawling datasets requires substantial hardware power, highlighting the urgent need for scalable processing techniques.

The computational burden is aslo equaly apparent in the medical industry, particularly within the domain of survival analysis. Survival analysis evaluates the time until a specific event of interest occurs, such as the onset of a disease or patient mortality \cite{Cox1972} . A fundamental component of this analysis is the concept of the \textit{risk set} this is a pool of subjects who are still event free and under observation at any given point in time. As the size of a clinical cohort grows, the mathematical risk set grows proportionally. In modern deep survival models, comparing an individual experiencing an event against the entire available risk set scales quadratically ($O(N^2)$), turning the evaluation of large datasets into a severe computational bottleneck.

This challenge is further compounded in real-world clinical settings by the presence of \textit{competing risks} \cite{latouche2013competing}. In a competing risks scenario, a patient may experience an alternative, mutually exclusive event that permanently precludes the primary event of interest from ever occurring (for example, a patient passing away from a non-cardiovascular cause before they can develop coronary heart disease) \cite{andersen2012competing}. To accurately model the cumulative incidence of the primary event without introducing statistical bias, models commonly rely on the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard framework \cite{FineGray1999}. However, the Fine-Gray formulation mathematically forces subjects who experience a competing event to remain in the risk set indefinitely, adjusted via censoring weights. As a result, the risk set does not organically shrink over time as it would in a standard Cox model; instead, it remains dense and massive, making full-risk training computationally paralyzing for deep neural networks \cite{Katzman2018, Lee2018}.

As deep survival architectures become more complex to capture non-linear clinical relationships \cite{kvamme2019}, their training durations have increased drastically. This extended training time is highly detrimental to the machine learning lifecycle. Model development is an inherently iterative process: an architecture must be trained before it can be evaluated, its predictions tested, and its hyperparameters tuned. If a single training run takes an excessive amount of time, or if a model trains incorrectly and must be restarted, the entire research, evaluation, and deployment pipeline is delayed. Speeding up the training phase directly accelerates the testing and validation cycles.

To resolve this, this thesis investigates the application of risk set sampling techniques such as Nested Case Control, Static Case-Cohort and the study's own dynamic sampling strategy to deep competing-risk models. By shifting away from training on the exhaustive full-cohort risk set and instead training on a strategically sampled, information-dense approximation of the cohort, it is possible to drastically accelerate model training \cite{Breslow1974, Prentice1986, Borgan2000}. Furthermore, as the world faces increasing constraints on global computing resources, memory bandwidth, and CPU/RAM availability in 2026, the ability to train robust deep learning models while dramatically reducing memory footprints and processing overhead is no longer just a convenience it is an operational necessity.

This study proposes that through the use of advanced, dynamic sampling techniques, deep survival models can maintain their statistical validity and predictive parity with full-risk models, all while achieving vital computational efficiency. By establishing a framework that samples intelligently rather than exhaustively, this research aims to streamline the evaluation of competing risks in large-scale medical data.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}

\subsection{Research Questions}
This study investigates the effects of risk-set sampling on competing risks for right-censored data, along with a custom dynamic sampling technique. The key research questions guiding this thesis are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How do case-cohort and nested case-control sampling designs affect discrimination, calibration, and explained variation in Fine-Gray models for competing risks compared to full risk analyses?
    \item How does the proposed dynamic sampling strategy, which adapts dynamically, affect discrimination, calibration, and explained variation in Fine-Gray models for competing risks compared to full risk analyses as well as the other techniques (NCC \& SCC)?
    \item What network and training design choices (e.g., additive interpretable modules, weight stabilization, censoring corrections, mini-batch risk-set sampling) ensure stable and consistent training on sampled data?
    \item Between case-cohort, nested case-control, and the study's own dynamic sampling strategy designs, which provides superior performance in terms of discrimination (C-index), Brier score, Integrated Brier score, robustness to censoring, and computational efficiency (CPU Usage, Memory Usage, and Training Durations)?
    \item How do different sampling strategies affect CPU usage, memory requirements, and training duration for deep Fine-Gray models?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Research Objectives}
To address the research questions outlined above, this study pursues the following primary objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To implement interpretable deep Fine-Gray models (CRISP-NAM) with stabilized inverse probability weighting censored (IPWC) for sampled risk sets using case-cohort, nested case-control, and the proposed dynamic sampling strategy.
    \item To evaluate and compare the predictive performance (discrimination, calibration, and explained variation) of full-cohort, case-cohort, nested case-control, and dynamic sampling designs using right-censored survival data.
    \item To evaluate the proposed dynamic sampling strategy in terms of predictive performance (discrimination, calibration) using C-index and Brier score against the full risk-set model as a statistical baseline, as well as robustness and computational efficiency (CPU usage, memory footprint, and training time), compared to case-cohort and nested case-control methods.
    \item To analyze the impact of different sampling strategies on computational efficiency, including CPU usage, memory requirements, and training duration across model configurations.
    \item To analyze the impact of different sampling strategies on the C-index, Brier Score, and Integrated Brier Score across model configurations.
    \item To provide practical guidelines for network design and training strategies that maintain interpretability, calibration, stability, and computational efficiency when using sampled risk sets.
    \item To produce reproducible code and benchmark results to support the adoption of sampling-aware deep survival analysis methods.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background Theory}
\label{ch:background_theory}

This chapter establishes the theoretical foundations required to model time to event data in the presence of multiple, mutually exclusive outcomes. It first reviews the mathematical challenges introduced by competing risks and evaluates classical statistical frameworks, including cause-specific and subdistribution hazards. The chapter then explores recent advancements in deep survival analysis, detailing how neural architectures can be adapted for competing events. Finally, it introduces the CRISPNAM framework and the risk set sampling strategies necessary for computationally efficient model optimization.

\section{Competing Risks in Survival Analysis}
While traditional survival analysis focuses on predicting risk for a single event of interest, many real-world problems such as death or getting infected, particularly in the healthcare domain, involve more than one competing event of interest. For example, patients who are at risk of death due to a specific disease may pass away following other unrelated complications, which acts as a competing risk that must be taken into account when predicting the primary risk. Conventional survival analysis approaches, including the standard Kaplan-Meier estimator and the Cox proportional hazards model \cite{Cox1972}, are inappropriate for competing risk scenarios because they erroneously handle alternative events as standard censored observations. This fundamental misclassification artificially alters the risk pool, resulting in inflated estimates of event probabilities and absolute risk. 

\section{Past Deep Neural Network Models}
There have been a steady growth in the advances in the area of deep learning have demonstrated improved predictive performance for survival analysis by replacing rigid parametric assumptions with flexible neural architectures capable of capturing complex, non-linear patterns in the data \cite{LeCun2015}. A foundational model in this domain is DeepSurv \cite{Katzman2018}, which successfully extended the traditional Cox proportional hazards framework by replacing the standard linear predictor with a deep neural network. The network learns the relationship between a patient's covariates and their risk by optimizing the standard Cox partial-likelihood objective \cite{Katzman2018}. Building upon this neural-Cox integration, Kvamme et al. \cite{kvamme2019} further advanced continuous-time survival predictions by developing neural networks that relax the strict proportional hazards assumption, enabling the model to explicitly learn complex and time-varying feature effects. However, while highly effective for standard survival settings, models like DeepSurv and the architectures proposed by Kvamme et al. are primarily designed for single-event survival and do not natively accommodate the mathematical complexities of competing risks.

To address multiple event types, methodologies such as DeepHit \cite{Lee2018} have been developed. DeepHit presents a joint modeling framework that employs a shared representation network coupled with cause-specific sub-networks to model the joint distribution of event time and type. The model optimization intelligently combines negative log-likelihood with a ranking loss to ensure concordance between predicted and observed outcomes \cite{Lee2018}. While DeepHit achieves strong predictive gains in competing risk scenarios, these gains come at the cost of interpretability, resulting in a black-box model that is difficult for clinicians to trust and validate in clinical practice.

\section{Cause Specific Hazard}
One of the most classical methodologies for estimation in competing risks survival analysis is the Cause Specific Hazard method, originally proposed by Prentice et al. \cite{prentice1978analysis}. Building directly upon the foundational Cox proportional hazards framework \cite{Cox1972}, this method models competing events separately by treating each outcome as a distinct hazard function. Under this formulation, the model evaluates the instantaneous rate of the primary event, conditional on the patient having survived completely event-free up to that time. 

For each cause $k$, the cause-specific hazard $\lambda_k(t|x)$ is mathematically defined as the instantaneous risk of experiencing event type $k$ at time $t$, given that the individual has survived without any event up until time $t$:
$$\lambda_k(t|x) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t+\Delta t, E = k \mid T \ge t, x)}{\Delta t}$$

Traditionally, the cause-specific hazard function is used in conjunction with the Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF) to calculate the risk score and determine the probability of event $k$ occurring over time. The CIF represents the probability that event type $k$ occurs by time $t$, accounting for the presence of competing risks. It is computed by integrating the cause-specific hazard weighted by the overall event-free survival function ($S(s|x)$):
$$F_k(t|x) = \int_0^t \lambda_k(s|x) S(s|x) ds$$

However, these classical approaches including standard Cox proportional hazards models heavily rely on risk-set definitions that erroneously treat alternative events as censored observations \cite{Cox1972}. This is problematic because it equates competing risks with standard right censoring. Right censoring is the most common form of censoring found in medical and reliability survival analysis, that is when the event of interest has not been observed for an individual during the study period \cite{kalbfleisch2002statistical}. In other words, we know the patient survived at least up to a certain point in time, but their exact outcome or the exact time of an event after that remains unknown. 

Consequently, subjects experiencing competing events are permanently removed from the risk set and treated mathematically as these right-censored observations \cite{latouche2013competing}. While computationally straightforward, cause-specific approaches impose strict independence assumptions. By treating competing events merely as censoring, the method fails to capture the true risk dynamics, ultimately leading to inflated and biased estimates of absolute event probabilities when competing events are dependent or when precise absolute risk predictions are required \cite{pintilie2006competing, andersen2012competing}.


\section{Fine-Gray Subdistribution Hazard}

To properly estimate absolute risk without the biases inherent to the cause-specific model, the Fine-Gray subdistribution method was introduced \cite{FineGray1999}. This method directly models the subdistribution hazard, maintaining subjects who experience competing events in the risk set. 

As an example of this contrast, in standard Cox models, competing events are treated as censored and the individuals are removed from the risk set. In the Fine-Gray model, individuals who have experienced competing events remain in the risk set but with a modified contribution. The subdistribution risk pool includes both subjects who are event-free at time $t$ and those who experienced a competing event before time $t$. Because these patients can no longer experience the primary event of interest, their influence in the risk pool is adjusted over time through Inverse Probability of Censoring Weights (IPCW), utilizing the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the censoring distribution.

The subdistribution hazard is denoted in two terms:
$$\tilde{\lambda}_k(t|X)=\tilde{\lambda}_{k,0}(t)\exp(\beta^\top X)$$
where $\tilde{\lambda}_{k,0}(t)$ is the baseline subdistribution hazard, which is used to capture the time dependence of the event $k$, and $\exp(\beta^\top X)$ is the relative risk of the individual based on the covariates $X$.

By keeping these patients in the risk pool, the Fine-Gray model preserves the probability structure induced by competing outcomes, enabling the direct modeling of the Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF). As we can see again, we have a CIF with the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard that accomplishes the same task as with the cause-specific hazard function: to calculate the probability that event $k$ occurs by time $t$ while accommodating for the competing risk. It is mathematically expressed as:
$$F_k(t|X) = P(T \le t, \Delta=k|X)$$

A key feature of the Fine-Gray approach is the use of a modified risk set, denoted by an indicator such as $Y_i^*(t)$. This indicator differs from standard survival analysis because it retains individuals who have experienced competing events, rather than removing them entirely. The weighted risk contribution describes how the Fine-Gray model applies appropriate weighting within the risk set to ensure correct representation in the estimation procedure, accounting for individuals who are censored or have experienced competing events. 

The individuals are then put in the modified risk set that is captured through $Y_i^*(t)$. It is utilized to compute $\bar{x}(t; \beta)$, which represents the risk-set weighted average of the covariates at time $t$. It is calculated over the modified risk set using IPCW weights $w_i(t)$ proportional to the relative risk $\exp(\beta^\top X)$, and it appears in the score function used to estimate the regression parameters:
$$\bar{x}(t; \beta)=\frac{\sum_i Y_i^*(t) w_i(t) X_i \exp(\beta^\top X_i)}{\sum_i Y_i^*(t) w_i(t) \exp(\beta^\top X_i)}$$


\section{CRISPNAM}

While traditional deep learning approaches offer high predictive capacity, their inherent "black-box" nature often limits clinical interpretability. A foundational work shaping the theoretical framework of this thesis is the approach by Ramachandram et al. \cite{InterpretableFineGray2025}, which introduced an interpretable Fine-Gray deep survival model designed specifically for competing risks. Building upon this vital need for transparency in deep survival analysis, the CRISPNAM architecture leverages a Neural Additive Model (NAM) framework to achieve both high flexibility and strict interpretability.

Central to the CRISPNAM architecture is the use of the FeatureNet. Unlike standard fully connected neural networks that process all inputs simultaneously through hidden layers, the additive framework assigns a dedicated, independent multi-layer perceptron a FeatureNet to each individual covariate. Let $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p)$ represent the set of $p$ input features. Each feature $x_j$ is passed exclusively through its own FeatureNet, denoted as $f_j(x_j)$, which learns a specific non-linear shape function for that variable alone. 

The additive framework mathematically combines these independent learned functions by summing them together to construct the overall risk predictor $f(X)$:
$$f(X) = \sum_{j=1}^p f_j(x_j)$$

This formulation elegantly integrates with the Fine-Gray method. By replacing the traditional linear predictor $\beta^\top X$ with the learned additive neural predictor $f(X)$, the modified subdistribution hazard becomes:
$$\tilde{\lambda}_k(t|X) = \tilde{\lambda}_{k,0}(t)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^p f_j(x_j)\right)$$

Because the model computes the hazard additively, the exact contribution of any single feature $x_j$ to the overall relative risk can be isolated and visualized. This architecture allows the model to capture complex, non-linear biostatistical relationships while retaining the exact interpretability of traditional regression models.


\section{Risk Set Sampling Strategies}

While the Fine-Gray modified risk set mathematically solves the statistical bias of competing risks, retaining individuals indefinitely creates a severe computational burden. Evaluating the full risk set at every event time scales quadratically, becoming a severe bottleneck for large datasets \cite{Wang2020}. To mitigate this, traditional biostatistics employ risk set sampling designs to computationally approximate the full cohort. 

One such method is Nested Case-Control (NCC) sampling, a time-matched design where, for each observed case $i$ at a given event time $t_i$, a fixed quota of $m$ controls is sampled uniformly without replacement from the valid risk set active at that specific moment, denoted as $R(t_i)$. Because controls are matched sequentially to specific events, it creates a much smaller, localized approximation of the risk set, $\tilde{R}(t_i)$. The probability of a control $j$ being included fluctuates depending entirely on the varying size of the full risk set at that time, $n(t_i)$. This inclusion probability is mathematically expressed as:
$$P(j \in \tilde{R}(t_i) \mid j \in R(t_i), j \neq i) = \frac{m}{n(t_i) - 1}$$

Alternatively, the Static Case-Cohort (SCC) design operates independently of event times by selecting a global subcohort $C$ of patients randomly at baseline (time zero) using a fixed sampling fraction $\alpha$ \cite{Prentice1986}. This static subcohort is then reused throughout the entirety of the training process. At any given event time $t_i$, the evaluated risk set consists of the current case $i$ along with any members of the baseline subcohort who are still actively at risk:
$$\tilde{R}(t_i) = \{i\} \cup (C \cap R(t_i))$$
Under this design, the probability of any individual being initially selected into the subcohort is strictly determined by the fixed baseline fraction:
$$P(j \in C) = \alpha$$

\section{Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) and Stabilization}

While sub-sampling architectures such as nested case-control and case-cohort designs significantly alleviate computational overhead, they fundamentally alter the underlying data distribution by over representing cases relative to controls \cite{Prentice1986, Borgan2000}. A naive analysis of this skewed subset would yield biased predictions. To rectify this sampling bias, Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) is applied \cite{HorvitzThompson1952}. 

This technique scales the contribution of each selected observation by the reciprocal of its sampling probability, allowing the subset to accurately mirror the full cohort. If $\pi_i$ represents the probability that individual $i$ is selected into the sample, the standard IPW is expressed mathematically as:
$$w_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}$$
Consequently, individuals who possess a lower likelihood of being sampled are assigned proportionally larger weights. This mechanism ensures that the sampled cohort yields consistent and unbiased estimates of the true population parameters \cite{Prentice1986, Borgan2000}.

However, a critical vulnerability of standard IPW arises when inclusion probabilities ($\pi_i$) become exceedingly small, generating disproportionately large weights. In the context of machine learning and gradient-based optimization, this extreme variability introduces significant numerical instability and severely inflates the variance of the estimator. To counter this, statistical and causal inference frameworks frequently employ weight stabilization \cite{Robins2000, Hernan2000}. By scaling the baseline weight by the overall marginal probability of selection into the sample, $P(S=1)$, the stabilized weight is defined as:
$$w_i^{\text{stab}} = \frac{P(S=1)}{\pi_i}$$
This mathematical normalization centers the weights and drastically curtails empirical variance. It preserves the bias-correcting properties of standard IPW while ensuring smoother convergence and computational stability during neural network training.

Beyond intentional sampling disparities, survival datasets are routinely subject to right-censoring, where the exact time of the event is not recorded for all individuals. To handle subjects whose true event times are partially unobserved, the framework incorporates Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) \cite{Graf1999}. IPCW dynamically adjusts the contribution of subjects based on the inverse of their probability of remaining uncensored up to a specific time, a probability that is typically derived from a censoring survival estimator, denoted as $\hat{G}(t|X)$. 

By integrating both stabilization and IPCW into the objective function, the model guarantees that inferences remain statistically valid and reflective of the true population dynamics, even when trained on partially observed and heavily sub-sampled data.

\section{Data Preprocessing}

To evaluate the robustness, accuracy, and interpretability of the competing risks models, experiments were conducted across three distinct data environments: a fully controlled synthetic dataset, the clinical Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) dataset, and a semi-synthetically modified version of the Framingham Heart Study dataset.

\subsection{Synthetic Data Generation}
A fully synthetic dataset was computationally generated to provide a controlled environment where the true underlying data generating distributions, baseline hazards, and covariate coefficients were known definitively. This ground-truth knowledge allows for exact empirical validation of the model's ability to recover true risk estimators without the confounding variables inherent to real-world medical data.

A cohort of $N = 15,000$ independent samples was generated. To ensure full experimental reproducibility, a fixed global random state (seed = 42) was utilized throughout the generation process. The feature space was constructed using 12 covariates: six continuous variables drawn from a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and six binary variables sampled from a binomial distribution $B(1, 0.5)$. These were concatenated to form the complete covariate matrix $X$.

Two competing events were simulated. For each event type $k \in \{1, 2\}$, a specific vector of true coefficients $\beta_k$ was predefined. To test the model's capacity for intrinsic feature selection and to simulate uninformative clinical noise, several covariates in both $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ were deliberately assigned a true coefficient of zero. The linear predictors for each individual were calculated as $\eta_{ik} = X_i^\top \beta_k$.

The cause-specific hazards were then defined exponentially as:
$$\lambda_k(t|X_i) = \lambda_{0,k} \exp(\eta_{ik})$$
where the baseline hazards were fixed at $\lambda_{0,1} = 0.01$ and $\lambda_{0,2} = 0.05$. 

The total instantaneous hazard for any event occurring was computed as the sum of the cause-specific hazards ($\lambda_{\text{total}} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$). The exact time to event for each subject was simulated via inverse transform sampling, drawing from an exponential distribution parameterized by the total hazard:
$$T_{\text{event}} = \frac{-\ln(U)}{\lambda_{\text{total}}}$$
where $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. If an event occurred, the specific event type (1 or 2) was assigned probabilistically using a binomial trial, where the probability of experiencing Event 1 was strictly proportional to its relative contribution to the total hazard ($\lambda_1 / \lambda_{\text{total}}$).

Finally, right-censoring was introduced independently to mimic study dropout or administrative study end. Censoring times, $T_{\text{censor}}$, were drawn from an exponential distribution with a predefined censoring rate of $0.01$. The final observed survival time was derived as the minimum of the simulated event time and the censoring time ($\tilde{T} = \min(T_{\text{event}}, T_{\text{censor}})$), and the event indicator was recorded as 0 if the individual was censored, or assigned the respective event type otherwise.

\subsection{Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) Dataset}
The Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) dataset, sourced from the Mayo Clinic trial, provides a classical real-world baseline for evaluating competing risks. Data preprocessing for this cohort initially required standardizing the temporal scale; observation times were converted from years into days to provide a higher-resolution continuous time axis for the neural network. 

The clinical outcomes were mapped into a defined competing risks state space: State 0 denoted right-censored patients, State 1 denoted death (the primary event of interest), and State 2 denoted liver transplantation (the competing event). Addressing missingness in the clinical covariates was crucial for tensor continuity. Missing continuous variables (such as serum cholesterol or prothrombin time) were imputed using the cohort mean. Categorical covariates were imputed using the mode (the most frequent class) prior to being transformed via one-hot encoding. Finally, redundant administrative identifiers and any medical records lacking valid, positive survival times were systematically excluded to establish the final analytical cohort.

\subsection{Framingham Heart Study Dataset}
To evaluate the models on a highly dimensional and well-understood cardiovascular cohort, the standard Framingham Heart Study dataset was adapted. Initial data cleaning involved resolving missing values via median imputation, ensuring that feature distributions were not severely skewed by outliers. 

Because standard Framingham data is often structured for binary classification rather than time-to-event analysis, the original deterministic target column (\texttt{ten\_year\_c\_h\_d}) was explicitly dropped from the feature matrix to eliminate any risk of data leakage during model optimization. As the baseline dataset lacked precise longitudinal tracking for multiple event types, time-to-event data was semi-synthetically generated to classify patient trajectories into three mutually exclusive states.

Event 1 (The Main Event) represented patients who actively developed Coronary Heart Disease (CHD). Their time-to-event was distributed randomly within the 10-year observational window. To induce a mathematically valid competing risk scenario, Event 2 (The Competing Event) was engineered to simulate a scenario where approximately 15\% of the non-CHD patients experienced a terminal occurrence such as a fatal accident or non-cardiovascular mortality prior to the 10-year mark. 

This semi-synthetic modification is vital for assessing the Fine-Gray loss function, as experiencing this alternative Event 2 permanently precludes the patient from ever experiencing the main CHD event, fundamentally shifting the risk set dynamics. Finally, Event 0 (Censoring) captured the remaining baseline patients who either safely survived event-free to the end of the 10 year study or were randomly lost to follow-up.

\section{Risk Set Sampling}

\subsection{Full Risk}
In standard survival models, computing the objective function requires evaluating the full risk set at every unique event time. When analyzing massive clinical datasets, this exhaustive evaluation becomes computationally prohibitive. To alleviate this bottleneck, various sampling architectures are implemented to condense the risk set at any given time step, actively reducing the number of subjects processed while theoretically preserving the integrity of the Fine-Gray loss function. By randomly drawing a subset of controls to accompany the observed case at time $t_j$ from the valid pool of subjects where $T_i \ge t_j$ \cite{Breslow1974}, computational complexity is drastically reduced while maintaining the localized, time-dependent probability structure of the subdistribution hazard.

\subsection{Case Cohort}
The static case-cohort design offers an alternative that operates independently of event times. In this architecture, a fixed subcohort is randomly selected at baseline (time zero) and systematically reused across the entire training lifecycle. While cases are universally retained when they experience the event of interest, the controls are exclusively drawn from this baseline subcohort. Consequently, this introduces uniform, yet unequal, inclusion probabilities where every member of the subcohort shares a constant probability of selection, denoted by a fixed sampling fraction $\pi_i = f$. To rectify the inherent selection bias caused by over representing cases, these fixed probabilities are integrated directly into an inverse probability weighted formulation of the Fine-Gray objective.

\subsection{Nested Case Control}
Conversely, nested case-control sampling employs a time-dependent matching strategy. At each precise event time $t_j$, the observed case is paired with a specific quota of $m$ controls, which are drawn strictly from the active risk set at that exact moment. Because the size of the risk set shrinks over time as patients experience events or are censored, the probability of a control being sampled, denoted as $\pi_{i,j}$, dynamically fluctuates. To ensure the sampled subset unbiasedly mimics the full cohort's structural contribution, these time-varying inclusion probabilities must be explicitly neutralized via inverse probability weighting within the partial likelihood function.

\subsection{Dynamic Sampling Strategy}
Moving beyond classical methodologies, this thesis introduces an adaptive, entropy-guided sampling framework applied directly to the subdistribution risk set. In the context of the Fine-Gray model, the modified risk pool at time $T_i$, denoted $\tilde{R}(T_i)$, mathematically retains individuals who have already experienced competing events, adjusting their influence via Inverse Probability of Censoring Weights (IPCW), $w_j(T_i)$.

This dynamic strategy begins by partitioning the active risk set into distinct groups $\{G_g(T_i)\}_{g=1}^{G_i}$ based on shared covariate profiles, represented by a covariate vector $\boldsymbol{X}_g$. The cumulative effective size of any specific group is calculated by summing its censoring weights:
$$W_g(T_i) = \sum_{j \in G_g(T_i)} w_j(T_i)$$
The empirical proportion of that group relative to the entire modified risk set is then calculated as:
$$p_g(T_i) = \frac{W_g(T_i)}{\sum_h W_h(T_i)}$$

To intelligently target informative yet potentially rare clinical profiles while leveraging the model's current predictive state, an importance score is generated for each group. This score fuses Shannon information gain with the model's current risk assessment:
$$s_g(T_i) = \bigl[-\log p_g(T_i)\bigr]\exp(\hat{\eta}(\boldsymbol{X}_g))$$
where $\hat{\eta}(\boldsymbol{X}_g)$ acts as the current neural risk prediction.

Rather than forcing a rigid sampling quota like the nested case-control design, this approach scales the importance scores against the average group importance to dictate independent inclusion probabilities, bounded by a baseline retention rate $\rho \in (0, 1]$:
$$\pi_g(T_i) = \min \left( 1, \;\rho \cdot \frac{s_g(T_i)}{\frac{1}{G_i}\sum_{h=1}^{G_i} s_h(T_i)} \right)$$
Through this mechanism, highly informative groups ($s_g$ significantly exceeding the mean) are deterministically retained ($\pi_g = 1$), whereas redundant or statistically dense groups are aggressively down-sampled. Because the sampling volume is fluid, the computational batch size organically expands or contracts based on the current informational density of the risk set. 

The resulting sampled collection of groups is denoted as $\tilde{\mathcal{G}}(T_i)$. The contribution of this intelligently sampled subset to the partial likelihood is then estimated using IPW:
$$\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(T_i) = \sum_{g \in \tilde{\mathcal{G}}(T_i)} \frac{W_g(T_i)}{\pi_g(T_i)} \exp\bigl(\eta(\boldsymbol{X}_g)\bigr)$$
Assuming proper specification of inclusion probabilities, this dynamic estimator remains design-consistent. However, because $\pi_g(T_i)$ relies on the active model state $\hat{\eta}(\cdot)$, it functions as an adaptive estimator, intentionally trading slight bias for significant variance reduction and computational efficiency.

\subsection{IPW and Stability}
Fundamentally, all evaluated sampling strategies such as case-cohort, nested case-control, and the dynamic entropy desig are structurally unified through the Inverse Probability Weighting framework. In each design, a selected individual or group contributes to the final Fine-Gray loss inversely proportional to its likelihood of being sampled. 

Let $\pi_i$ represent the generalized inclusion probability for subject $i$ (or $\pi_g(t)$ for a group). The core mechanism of IPW dictates that, under correct probability specification, the weighted estimator seamlessly recovers the full-cohort objective in expectation. This is grounded in Horvitz-Thompson unbiasedness \cite{HorvitzThompson1952}, defined as:
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\{i \in S\}}{\pi_i} \, \phi_i \right] = \phi_i$$
where $\phi_i$ represents any individual contribution to the full dataset objective. This unifying mathematical principle ensures that regardless of how the data is subsetted whether statically, temporally, or dynamically the underlying risk estimates remain mathematically stable and reflective of the true clinical population.

\section{Network Architecture (CRISPNAM-FG)}
\subsection{Network architecture}

The modeling framework follows the CRISP-NAM design \cite{InterpretableFineGray2025}, extended here to accommodate mini-batch risk-set sampling under the competing-risks Fine Gray formulation. The architecture is grounded in a Neural Additive Model (NAM) structure, wherein each covariate acts on the sub-distribution hazard independently through its own dedicated sub-network, termed a \textit{FeatureNet}.

Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^p$ denote the vector of input covariates for a subject. Each scalar feature $x_i$ ($i = 1, \dots, p$) is mapped through a dedicated feedforward sub-network $f_i(x_i): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, yielding an isolated non-linear contribution to the cause-specific log-subdistribution hazard.

\subsubsection{FeatureNet Formulation}
Each FeatureNet $f_i(\cdot)$ is constructed as a deep multilayer perceptron comprising $L$ hidden layers. Setting the initial layer input to the scalar covariate $z_i^{(0)} = x_i$, the transformation proceeds recursively across hidden layers $l = 1, \dots, L$:
\begin{align}
    u_i^{(l)} &= W_i^{(l)} z_i^{(l-1)} + b_i^{(l)}, \\
    z_i^{(l)} &= \text{Dropout}\left( \text{SiLU}\left(u_i^{(l)}\right); p_{\text{drop}} \right), \quad l = 1, \dots, L,
\end{align}
where $W_i^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times d_{l-1}}$ and $b_i^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l}$ denote the layer weights and bias vectors, respectively, and $p_{\text{drop}}$ is the dropout retention rate. The sub-networks utilize the Sigmoid Linear Unit ($\text{SiLU}$) activation function, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SiLU}(u) = u \cdot \sigma(u) = \frac{u}{1 + e^{-u}}.
\end{equation}
The smooth, continuously differentiable, and non-monotonic nature of $\text{SiLU}$ mitigates vanishing gradients across deeper sub-networks while yielding smooth univariate feature effect curves.

The final representation $z_i^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ is projected to a scalar output via a bias-free linear transformation:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{f}_i(x_i) = W_i^{(L+1)} z_i^{(L)},
\end{equation}
where $W_i^{(L+1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_L}$. To guarantee model identifiability a core requirement of generalized additive models each FeatureNet enforces a zero-mean batch constraint during training:
\begin{equation}
    f_i(x_{b,i}) = \tilde{f}_i(x_{b,i}) - \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^B \tilde{f}_i(x_{j,i}),
\end{equation}
for sample $b$ within a mini-batch of size $B$. This centering operation ensures that individual shape functions $\mathbb{E}[f_i(x_i)] = 0$ capture purely relative variations, preventing arbitrary vertical translation between subnetworks.

\subsubsection{Global Additive Aggregation}
The overall model aggregates the individual feature-specific transformations additively with a learnable global intercept $\beta_0 \in \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{equation}
    \eta(\mathbf{x}) = \beta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p f_i(x_i),
\end{equation}
where $\eta(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the predicted log-subdistribution hazard score (relative risk). Because the final projection layers of the FeatureNets omits additive biases, the global baseline risk is entirely parameterized by $\beta_0$.

All linear layer weights are initialized using Xavier uniform initialization 
\begin{equation}
    W \sim \mathcal{U}\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}, \sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}\right)
\end{equation}

with biases initialized to zero. During training, the aggregated risk score $\eta(\mathbf{x})$ is optimized directly against the sampling-weighted Fine Gray partial likelihood loss. For post hoc model interpretation, each univariate shape function $f_i(x_i)$ can be visualized over its covariate support domain to inspect linear or non-linear effects on sub-distribution incidence.


\section{Loss Function}

To optimize the neural relative risk predictor under varying computational constraints, the network is trained by minimizing a sampling-aware extension of the Fine-Gray partial likelihood. This objective function is mathematically designed to integrate the additive neural predictor, correct for right-censored observations, and neutralize the selection bias introduced by risk-set sampling architectures. 

The formulation is constructed by first establishing the baseline Fine-Gray objective applied to the full cohort. Let $\mathcal{R}(T_i)$ denote the complete modified risk set evaluated at event time $T_i$ for the target cause $k$. To account for patients who are independently right-censored, Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) is applied \cite{Graf1999}. If $\hat{G}(t \mid \mathbf{x})$ represents the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the censoring survival distribution, the censoring weight for a subject $j$ at time $T_i$ is computed as:
$$ w_j^{\text{cens}}(T_i) = \frac{1}{\hat{G}(T_i^- \mid \mathbf{x}_j)} $$
Replacing the traditional linear predictor with the additive neural risk score $\eta_k(\mathbf{x})$, the baseline negative log-partial likelihood evaluated over the full risk set is formulated as:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FG}}(\beta) = -\sum_{i:\Delta_i = k} w_i^{\text{cens}}(T_i) \left[ \eta_k(\mathbf{x}_i) - \log \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}(T_i)} w_j^{\text{cens}}(T_i) \exp(\eta_k(\mathbf{x}_j)) \right] $$

While statistically sound, optimizing this full baseline objective across all event times is computationally exhaustive. When sub-sampling architectures (such as nested case-control or static case-cohort designs) are employed, the full risk set $\mathcal{R}(T_i)$ is replaced by a much smaller sampled subset, denoted as $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(T_i)$. However, this subsampling distorts the natural risk distribution. To recover an unbiased representation of the original cohort, Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) is applied based on the selection probabilities \cite{HorvitzThompson1952, Prentice1986}.

For a given subject $j$ with an inclusion probability of $\pi_j$, the naive IPW weight is $1/\pi_j$. Because extreme fractional probabilities can destabilize neural network gradients (Introducing varince in the weights of the model), a variance reduction technique is applied by scaling the weights by the empirical marginal probability of selection, $P(S=1)$ \cite{Robins2000, Hernan2000}. This yields the stabilized sampling weight:
$$ w_j^{\text{stab}} = \frac{P(S=1)}{\pi_j} $$

The global contribution weight for any subject $j$ evaluated at time $T_i$ is therefore the product of their stabilized sampling weight and their censoring weight:
$$ w_{ij} = w_j^{\text{stab}} \cdot w_j^{\text{cens}}(T_i) $$

By injecting these combined weights and evaluating only over the sampled subset $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}(T_i)$, the final sampling-aware Fine-Gray objective utilized to train the model is derived as:
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FG}}^{\text{IPW}}(\beta) = -\sum_{i:\Delta_i = k} w_{ii} \left[ \eta_k(\mathbf{x}_i) - \log \sum_{j \in \tilde{\mathcal{R}}(T_i)} w_{ij} \exp(\eta_k(\mathbf{x}_j)) \right] $$
where $w_{ii}$ serves as the combined weight of the observed case at its own event time. 

Notably, explicit weight clipping a technique frequently utilized in reinforcement learning and causal inference to bound extreme gradients is intentionally omitted from this framework. Because the sub sampled risk sets are already heavily reduced, artificially truncating valid IPW weights at arbitrary thresholds would discard critical representation information, re-introducing the very statistical bias the IPW formulation is designed to eliminate. 

Throughout the experimental phase of this thesis, the models were aggressively optimized against this sampling-aware $\mathcal{L}_{\text{FG}}^{\text{IPW}}$ loss function. To ensure robust validation and provide a rigorous experimental control, baseline models were trained alongside them using the exact same weighted loss formulation, but evaluated comprehensively over the full, unsampled risk set.


\section{Training Details}

To guarantee rigorous and unbiased evaluation across the varying datasets, the model's predictive capabilities and sampling mechanics were validated using a five-fold cross-validation framework. To address the inherent imbalance often found in survival datasets where the primary event of interest may be significantly overshadowed by censored observations or competing risks a Stratified K-Fold splitting strategy was implemented. By conditioning the fold generation directly on the event indicators, this event-aware stratification ensures that the proportion of competing events, primary events, and right-censored subjects remains strictly consistent across all training and validation splits. This prevents "fold collapse," a phenomenon where a validation set is accidentally starved of minority outcomes, which would severely skew loss gradients and validation metrics. 

Within this cross-validation structure, the experimental pipeline was designed to evaluate both full-batch and mini-batch optimization regimes. This dual regime approach allows for a direct empirical assessment of how disparate sampling architectures interact with varying computational constraints. Full batch training aggregates the partial likelihood across the entirety of the risk set (whether fully enumerated or sampled) before executing a single gradient update. This serves as the classical baseline, mirroring traditional biostatistical optimization. 

Conversely, mini-batch training iterates over strict, localized subsets of the cohort. This mechanism is crucial for scaling deep survival models to massive clinical databases by constraining memory overhead. To seamlessly integrate this into the PyTorch ecosystem, event aware DataLoaders were configured to stream batches while maintaining temporal and categorical stability. However, the convergence dynamics of mini-batching in a competing risks environment are statistically non-trivial. When integrated with aggressive risk-set condensation techniques such as static case-cohort, nested case-control, or the proposed dynamic entropy sampling the neural network is updated using only a fragmented fraction of an already deeply sub-sampled risk pool. 

Consequently, a core objective of this training protocol is to investigate whether this compounded sparsity degrades the stability of the Fine-Gray loss function, or if the Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) mechanism is robust enough to maintain convergence and predictive accuracy under stochastic mini-batch conditions. This comparative analysis isolates whether sampling alone provides sufficient computational acceleration, or if true scalability requires the hybridization of sampling and mini-batching.

All model configurations were optimized utilizing the Adam optimizer \cite{kingma2014adam}. Adam is highly effective in deep survival contexts as it leverages adaptive learning rates and moving averages of historical gradients to navigate the complex loss topographies generated by IPW scaling. To prevent the FeatureNets from memorizing localized noise a heightened risk when training on deeply sub-sampled risk sets L2 parameter regularization (weight decay) was integrated into the optimization loop. This regularizes the network by penalizing excessively large weights, promoting smoother feature effect curves and ensuring that the interpretable sub-networks generalize effectively to unseen clinical populations.


\section{Model Evaluation and Diagnostics}

This section details the evaluation metrics and diagnostic frameworks employed to validate the predictive performance, calibration, and computational efficiency of the competing risks models. To provide a holistic assessment, the models are evaluated on their discriminative capacity using a time-dependent Concordance Index, their absolute probabilistic accuracy via the Brier and Integrated Brier Scores, and their clinical reliability through Stratified Aalen-Johansen Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF) calibration. Furthermore, computational profiling and synthetic correlation metrics are introduced to explicitly validate the theoretical efficiency and exact feature recovery of the proposed CRISPNAM architecture.

\subsection{Brier Score}
In standard regression tasks, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) evaluates the distance between a prediction and the continuous truth. To quantify absolute probabilistic accuracy under competing risks, the Brier Score serves as the equivalent metric, formulated directly against the Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF). Evaluated at a specific clinical time horizon $t$, the Brier Score calculates the mean squared difference between the model's predicted probability of the primary event $k$ occurring prior to time $t$, and the actual observed binary outcome.

Because survival data is subject to right censoring meaning the exact outcome is unknown for some patients at time $t$ the naive Brier Score is heavily biased. To resolve this, an Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) term, $w_i(t)$, derived from the Kaplan-Meier censoring survival function $\hat{G}(\cdot)$, is dynamically applied. For a cohort of $N$ patients, the adjusted Brier Score is defined as:
$$\text{BS}(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w_i(t) \left( \mathbf{1}[T_i \le t, \Delta_i = k] - \hat{F}_k(t \mid \mathbf{x}_i) \right)^2$$
where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function denoting whether the specific event $k$ was observed prior to time $t$. A lower Brier Score reflects superior calibration and tighter probabilistic agreement with empirical reality.

\subsection{Integrated Brier Score (IBS)}
While the point-in-time Brier Score is useful for specific clinical horizons (e.g., the median follow-up time), it is highly dependent on the chosen evaluation point and fails to capture the model's accuracy over the entire temporal trajectory. To resolve this, the Integrated Brier Score (IBS) is utilized as a global summary metric. 

The IBS computes the average of the Brier Score evaluated continuously over a prespecified temporal interval $[0, \tau]$:
$$\text{IBS} = \frac{1}{\tau} \int_0^{\tau} \text{BS}(t) \, dt$$
By integrating the predictive error across all valid time steps, the IBS collapses short-term and long-term probabilistic accuracy into a single scalar value. This metric is critical for objectively comparing disparate sampling strategies across the entire follow-up period.

\subsection{Time-Dependent Concordance Index (C-Index)}
While the Brier Score measures absolute probabilistic error, the Concordance Index (C-Index) quantifies the model's discriminative ability by evaluating how accurately the predicted relative risk scores rank the observed event times. In a clinical setting, discrimination answers the question: \textit{If Patient A experiences a fatal cardiac event before Patient B, did the model assign Patient A a higher risk score?}

Let $\eta_i$ and $\eta_j$ denote the predicted log-risk scores for a pair of subjects. A pair is considered concordant if the subject who fails earlier was assigned a higher risk score ($\eta_i > \eta_j$). Because right-censoring obscures the true temporal ordering for certain patients, an IPCW adjustment is integrated to ensure unbiased evaluation. To properly evaluate non-proportional hazards where risk predictions vary over time, we rely on the time-dependent concordance index proposed by Antolini et al. \cite{Antolini2005}. Adapted for time-dependent and competing risks scenarios, the estimator is defined as:
$$\widehat{C}_{\text{IPCW}} = \frac{ \sum_{i \neq j} \mathbf{1}(T_i < T_j, \Delta_i = k) \mathbf{1}(\eta_i > \eta_j) / \hat{G}^2(T_i) }{ \sum_{i \neq j} \mathbf{1}(T_i < T_j, \Delta_i = k) / \hat{G}^2(T_i) }$$
A value of $0.5$ indicates performance equivalent to random chance, whereas a value approaching $1.0$ proves strong clinical discriminative rank-ordering.

\subsection{Stratified Aalen-Johansen CIF Calibration}
Strong discrimination (a high C-Index) guarantees that the model correctly ranks patient risk, but it does not guarantee calibration. Calibration asks: \textit{If the model predicts a 20\% risk of an event for a group of patients, do approximately 20\% of those patients actually experience the event?} To prove that the absolute risk estimations output by the Fine-Gray loss are reliable, a Stratified CIF calibration diagnostic was developed.

Under this diagnostic, the validation cohort is stratified into discrete quantiles (e.g., Low, Medium, and High risk) based on the model's predicted relative risk scores. For each stratum, the mean predicted CIF curve is plotted against empirical reality. Because the standard Kaplan-Meier estimator is mathematically invalid in the presence of competing risks, the empirical reality is estimated strictly using the non-parametric Aalen-Johansen estimator \cite{aalen1978empirical}. If the model's predicted mean CIF curve closely shadows the step-wise Aalen-Johansen empirical curve across all risk strata, it proves that the neural network's absolute risk estimates are highly calibrated.

\subsection{CPU and Memory Profiling}
A fundamental hypothesis of this thesis is that sub-sampling the risk set via static, nested, or dynamic entropy-guided designs dramatically reduces the computational burden inherent to the full cohort Fine-Gray objective. Beyond predictive accuracy, this study explicitly evaluates the computational efficiency of these sampling regimes.

A continuous resource profiler is executed alongside the cross-validation training loop. Operating on an isolated parallel thread, the profiler polls the system to record absolute CPU utilization and Resident Set Size (RSS) memory consumption, ensuring that only the resources directly consumed by the model training process are captured. By measuring resource trajectories and total execution durations independently of external background noise, the study accurately quantifies the exact computational efficiency gains unlocked by the risk set approximation methods.

\subsection{Synthetic Data Verification (Pearson \& Spearman Correlation)}
A primary limitation of analyzing real-world medical data is the absence of an absolute "ground truth" for individual feature effects. While clinical literature can suggest trends, the exact mathematical coefficient for a specific covariate remains fundamentally unknown. Consequently, verifying whether an interpretable model has accurately isolated a specific feature's contribution is challenging.

To bypass this limitation, the synthetic cohort experiment incorporates Pearson and Spearman rank correlation coefficients. Because the synthetic dataset was generated with explicitly defined baseline hazards and exact true linear predictors, the ground truth is strictly known. The Pearson coefficient measures the degree of linear agreement between the model's predicted feature effects and the true parameters, while the Spearman coefficient evaluates the monotonic rank correlation. High correlation scores explicitly prove that the CRISPNAM architecture successfully recovers the true underlying biostatistical relationships without feature entanglement.

\section{Optimization and Convergence}

Training deep survival architectures necessitates careful monitoring to prevent overfitting, particularly when optimizing complex sub-distribution hazard formulations across varying risk-set sampling designs. To ensure robust evaluation, all models were trained using a stratified 5-fold cross-validation strategy, ensuring consistent underlying event proportions across all splits. 

Given the extensive hyperparameter search space across the three datasets and four sampling strategies, plotting every trajectory is impractical. Instead, the loss curves presented reflect a single representative fold (Fold 1) utilizing the optimal hyperparameter configuration for each sampling type. The full cohort loss serves as the foundational baseline against which the sampling strategies are compared.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Stability and Selection}

The convergence stability of the CRISPNAM architecture was highly dependent on the hyperparameter configurations of the risk-set samplers. The optimal parameters were selected based on the highest cross-validated Concordance Index, revealing several consistent behavioral patterns across the datasets.

Nested Case-Control (NCC) Sampling we have found that the optimal number of controls was found to be $m = 5$. Increasing the number of controls did not yield significant predictive improvements but linearly increased computational overhead. This aligns with standard literature on deep survival sampling, notably Kvamme et al., who demonstrated that minimal control sets are sufficient to approximate the partial likelihood gradient effectively.

Static Case-Cohort (SCC) Sampling where the subcohort fraction of $\alpha = 0.5$ provided the most stable optimization trajectory. While lower fractions ($\alpha \in [0.2, 0.3]$) ultimately achieved similar terminal loss values, their convergence curves were highly jagged. Extreme subcohort compression ($\alpha < 0.2$) led to gradient collapse during early epochs. Thus, $\alpha = 0.5$ is recommended for maintaining gradient stability.

The Entropy Sampling makes use of a temperature scaling parameter ($\tau$)  that controls the greediness of the sampler. The optimal balance was found at $\tau = 0.5$. When the temperature was pushed toward maximum greediness ($\tau = 0.1$), the model severely overfit, as it exclusively sampled the highest-variance patients and failed to generalize to the broader distribution. Based on these empirical trajectories, a range of $\tau \in [0.5, 1.0]$ is recommended to adequately balance the exploitation of high-information risk sets with robust model generalizability.

\subsection{Computational Resource Profiling}

A primary motivation for implementing risk-set sampling strategies in deep survival architectures is the reduction of computational overhead. Calculating the exact sub-distribution partial likelihood for the full cohort becomes computationally intractable as dataset sizes scale. To quantify this efficiency gain, hardware resource utilization (CPU and Memory) and execution times were profiled during model training. 

Given that the computational patterns were consistent across all experiments, Figure \ref{fig:resource_profiling} visualizes the resource profiles exclusively for the large-scale synthetic dataset ($N=15,000$), which serves as the upper-bound stress test for the architecture. The smaller PBC and Framingham datasets exhibited identical behavioral patterns at lower absolute magnitudes.

\subsubsection{Resource Utilization and Execution Efficiency}

Across all sampling configurations, CPU utilization remained highly stable at approximately 50\% during the active training phase, indicating consistent dataloader throughput without severe processing bottlenecks. 

Memory utilization exhibited a characteristic pattern across all methods: an initial allocation spike during data ingestion and model initialization, followed by a stable plateau during batched gradient updates. 

    \paragraph{Full Cohort} The baseline full cohort calculation required approximately 725 MB during initialization and plateaued near 600 MB. However, it required the longest execution time, taking roughly 60 seconds per fold iteration.
    \paragraph{Static Case-Cohort (SCC) \& Nested Case-Control (NCC)} Both SCC ($\alpha=0.5$) and NCC ($m=5$) required slightly higher initial memory spikes (approx. 790 MB and 780 MB, respectively) due to the overhead of the sampling algorithms allocating the risk-sets in memory. However, by compressing the active risk pool, both methods reduced the execution time to approximately 45 seconds a 25\% reduction in compute time compared to the full cohort.
    \paragraph{Entropy Sampling} The entropy sampler ($\tau=0.5$) exhibited the highest initialization memory peak (approx. 830 MB), reflecting the upfront computational cost of calculating the entropy distribution across the risk-sets. Strikingly, once initialized, this strategy proved to be the most efficient, completing the execution in roughly 28 seconds. This represents a greater than 50\% reduction in training time compared to the baseline, proving that intelligently exploiting high-information risk sets drastically accelerates optimization.


Ultimately, these profiles confirm that while advanced sampling strategies require marginally more initialization memory to construct the active cohorts, they heavily optimize the overall computational pipeline by drastically reducing the time required for gradient convergence.

\section{Real-World Model Calibration and Discrimination}

While the synthetic dataset provided a mathematically pure environment to test absolute boundaries and feature recovery, standard clinical data introduces high variance, unscaled covariates, and inherent observational noise. To validate the CRISPNAM architecture's generalizability, the calibration of the risk-set sampling mechanisms was evaluated against the real-world Primary Biliary Cirrhosis (PBC) benchmark.

Because the ground truth linear predictor is fundamentally unknown in real-world clinical data, the evaluation relies exclusively on empirical calibration diagnostics. Specifically, the model's predicted Cumulative Incidence Functions (CIF) are plotted against the non-parametric Aalen-Johansen estimators, evaluated both at a stratified group level and via marginal calibration gaps.

This final evaluation solidifies the overarching operational framework for the CRISPNAM architecture. Across both small, noisy clinical trials (PBC) and heavily censored longitudinal studies (Framingham), highly greedy entropy sampling ($\tau=0.5$) corrupts IPW calibration. However, by treating the temperature parameter $\tau$ as an environmental hyperparameter tuning it upward ($\tau \in [1.0, 5.0]$) to flatten the sampling distribution in the presence of severe data sparsity or noise the Entropy sampler successfully matches Full Cohort predictive power and absolute calibration at a fraction of the computational cost.
